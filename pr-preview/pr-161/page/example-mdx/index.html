Prerequisites

  • Comfort with probability on finite sets (E, distributions on a finite X).
  • One variable of calculus: we differentiate −tlogt once.
  • From Prerequisites (day 0): big-O notation and the idea of a bit.
What you’ll learn

What

  • Define the entropy of a distribution and compute it by hand for small examples.
  • State and prove Gibbs' inequality, and use it to bound entropy.
  • Recognise every component the page supports, and copy this file as the starting point for a sheet.

Why

Entropy is the unit of account for everything later in the course that measures information: description length, KL divergence, mutual information. A participant who cannot manipulate it fluently is stuck from day two onwards.

How

A morning lecture states the definitions and proves Gibbs' inequality; an exercise session makes the participants reprove it and find the equality case themselves; a reading and discussion block connects it to coding.

Lecture: entropy

Intent

Give the participants the definition and one inequality they can prove themselves, so the afternoon exercises are a rehearsal and not a first sighting. Everything after the proof of Gibbs' inequality can be cut if time is short.

Teaching note

TN, slide 12: draw −tlogt on the board before showing the plot; most participants have never seen that it is concave.

Definition (entropy). The entropy of a distribution p on a finite set X is

H(p)=−x∈X∑​p(x)logp(x).

Theorem (Gibbs' inequality). For any distributions p,q on X, −∑x​p(x)logq(x)≥H(p), with equality iff p=q.

Proof

By definition, DKL​(p∥q)=∑x​p(x)logq(x)p(x)​≥0 by Jensen's inequality applied to the concave function log; rearranging gives the claim. Equality in Jensen requires p(x)/q(x) constant, i.e. p=q.

Lemma. H(p)≤log∣X∣, with equality iff p is uniform.

Proof

Apply Gibbs' inequality with q uniform.

Example. A fair coin has H=log2: one bit. A coin with p(heads)=1 has H=0.

The idea of entropy is due to Shannon (1948); for a textbook treatment see MacKay (2003), chapter 4.

Exercise session

Intent

The participants reprove the lecture's inequality with their own hands and find its equality case, so that "entropy is maximised by the uniform distribution" becomes something they derived rather than heard. Pairs; the hard direction is the one to discuss on the board.

Exercise 1 (Entropy warm-up). Let p be a distribution on a finite set X.

(a) Show that H(p)≥0.

(b) For which p is H(p)=0?

Solution

For (a): each term −p(x)logp(x)≥0 since p(x)∈[0,1]. For (b): H(p)=0 iff every term vanishes iff p is a point mass. [Hint: when is −tlogt=0 on [0,1]?]

Exercise 2 (★) (Gibbs, the hard direction). Prove the equality case of Gibbs' inequality directly, without citing Jensen's equality condition.

Hint

Consider f(t)=t−1−logt: it is nonnegative on t>0 and vanishes only at t=1.

Solution

Write f(t)=t−1−logt for t>0 and note f(t)≥0 with equality iff t=1. Then with tx​=q(x)/p(x),

−x∑​p(x)logq(x)−H(p)=x∑​p(x)f(tx​)+x∑​(p(x)−q(x))≥0,

and equality forces tx​=1 wherever p(x)>0, i.e. p=q.

Exercise 3 (Open-ended). Why might a learning system that compresses its inputs well also predict them well? No solution is given: this is a discussion exercise.

Reading and discussion

Intent

Connect the morning's definition to coding, through one well-curated reading with clear take-aways, then a discussion that compares and contrasts. Groups of three to four; steer towards the source-coding theorem if the discussion stalls.

  • MacKay (2003), sections 4.1–4.3 (about 40 minutes). Take-away: the entropy is the average length of the best code.
  • Shannon (1948), section 6 only. Take-away: where the axioms for H come from.

Discussion prompt

Two participants disagree: one says entropy is a property of the source, the other that it is a property of the observer's beliefs about the source. Who is right, and does Gibbs' inequality settle it?
